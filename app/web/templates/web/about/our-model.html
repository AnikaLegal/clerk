Our model: how a small team
helps hundreds of renters

Renters face rising costs, unsafe homes and a legal system that is hard to navigate alone, and traditional services cannot meet the scale of that need. So we built a different way of working.

Who works on a case

Every case is assigned a law student paralegal, who is the renter's point of contact throughout. Their work is supervised by a lawyer, who guides them and signs off on all legal advice. Experienced paralegals triage new enquiries and provide support, and our Principal Lawyer oversees the whole practice.

This structure is how we can help more people. One full-time lawyer supervising students and secondees can support up to 300 renters a year. In 2024-25, four staff, 136 law students, 90 volunteers and a group of lawyers seconded from law firms delivered 508 legal services to 333 renters.

A model where everyone wins

Anika’s student-powered service model creates shared value – every participant benefits in a meaningful way:

  • Renters receive free, high-quality support when they need it most.
  • Students gain practical legal experience that enhances their employability.
  • Universities improve law student learning outcomes with minimal additional investment.
  • The community legal sector gains a scalable workforce pipeline and dramatically increased capacity.

This design unlocks value that already exists in the system — tens of thousands of law students seeking experience and millions of Australians needing legal help — and channels it into an efficient, high-impact service model.

Our university partnerships

Universities and education providers play a critical role in sustaining our service model. Law schools are increasingly required to provide authentic workplace learning, and we deliver it at scale, at low cost, and with strong student outcomes. In return, our education partners provide the funding and the recurring student capacity that let us grow.

This is our only commercial activity, and it was under a tenth of our income in 2024-25. It supports the mission directly rather than sitting alongside it: the students a university pays us to teach are the students who, supervised by our lawyers, get a renter's home repaired or their bond returned.

In 2024-25 we worked with RMIT University, Deakin University and Swinburne University of Technology.

How the work is funded

The service is paid for by a combination of philanthropic funding, earned revenue from the university partnerships, and a large amount of donated time and expertise.

Income in 2024-25 was $681,525, an increase of 46% year on year, reflecting strong support from philanthropic partners and continued growth in both corporate and education provider revenue. Of that, $65,400 came from the university partnerships.

Volunteers, seconded lawyers and pro bono partners donated services valued at $491,329, taking our total income to $1,172,853. That donated capacity is the equivalent of several extra staff at no extra cost, and it is what lets the model reach further than cash funding alone could pay for.

Where the money goes

Total expenditure in 2024-25 was $689,360, broadly in line with income. That reflects continued investment in staff, program delivery, partnerships, technology and infrastructure to meet rising demand for services. Employee costs were $616,077 of the total, close to nine dollars in every ten, which is what it costs to have a lawyer supervising every case. Including in-kind contributions, total expenditure was $1,180,689.

Our operating result for the year, excluding in-kind contributions, was -$211: close to break even in a year of deliberate growth.

Where these numbers come from

The financial figures are from our Special Purpose Financial Report for the year ended 30 June 2025, which has been independently reviewed and is prepared to meet the Australian Charities and Not-for-profits Commission Act 2012, the Victorian Fundraising Appeals Act 1998 and the Corporations Act 2001. The service figures are from our FY25 Impact Report.
